# Story Craft Reference

Use structure as a diagnostic tool, not a formula.

## Core story questions

A story architecture must answer:

- Who is the story about?
- What do they want in concrete terms?
- Why now?
- What blocks them?
- What do they risk losing?
- What mistaken strategy, belief, loyalty, fear, or blind spot makes the conflict harder?
- What changes because of the climax?
- What remains unresolved on purpose?

## Causality

A beat should cause, constrain, reveal, or reframe a later beat. Replace sequences held together only by "and then" with cause and consequence.

## Escalation

Escalation can increase cost, intimacy, exposure, time pressure, moral compromise, irreversibility, or loss of options. It does not require explosions or a larger antagonist.

## Scene value

A scene earns its place by changing at least one of these:

- goal
- knowledge
- relationship
- leverage
- danger
- commitment
- plan
- location or time in a way that matters

If nothing changes, combine, compress, or cut the scene.

## Scene and aftermath diagnostic

When a sequence feels mechanically episodic, test the action and aftermath separately. The action phase usually has a concrete goal, resistance, and a turn or adverse result. The aftermath usually contains reaction, a dilemma created by what just happened, and a decision that creates the next goal.

This is a diagnostic, not a mandatory template. Some scenes combine these functions, omit one intentionally, or cut directly from consequence to the next action. The useful test is whether emotional processing and the next causal choice are both legible when the story needs them.

## Character

Build characters from pressure behavior, contradictions, habits, limits, and relationships. Avoid biographies that never affect choices on the page.

## Ending

The ending should resolve the central dramatic question and show the cost of the resolution. It does not need to explain the theme.
